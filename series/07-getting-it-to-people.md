# Getting It to People: Aim, Story, Funding and Selling

*Part 7 of From a Human Need to a Working Company*

---

Peter Drucker wrote in 1954 that the purpose of a business is "to create a customer." That's why he said a business has two basic functions: marketing and innovation. Those two produce results, and everything else is a cost.

As an engineer, I used to think of selling as the step after the real work. Inside a company, it's half the work.

## Where to aim

Before building, ask six questions:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How much does it hurt? | People pay to stop pain before they pay for nice-to-haves |
| Can they pay? | A need with no money behind it is charity |
| Can you reach them? | A perfect customer you can't reach is useless |
| Is the market growing? | A growing market forgives mistakes |
| Why you? | What do you have that's hard to copy? |
| What do they use today? | Your real competitor is often a spreadsheet, or doing nothing |

One rule sits under all of them: **what's scarce gets paid, and what's abundant gets cheap.** AI is making code, writing and basic analysis abundant. Value is moving to what's still scarce: trust, judgment, taste, owning an outcome, and the ability to reach people.

## The story

Value nobody hears about doesn't exist. A good message answers five things, in order:

1. **Who:** one specific person, not everyone
2. **Pain:** their problem, in their own words
3. **Promise:** what changes for them
4. **Proof:** why they should believe you
5. **Path:** one clear next step

April Dunford adds a step before the message: **positioning.** Start with what customers would use if you didn't exist. Then name what you have that those alternatives don't, what value that creates, who cares most about it, and which category makes it obvious. If a message isn't landing, the problem is often the positioning underneath it.

## Proving people want it

Sean Ellis created a simple test. Ask your users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. In his experience across many startups, the ones that grew had roughly **40% or more** answer "very disappointed."

Below that point, spending money on growth mostly buys customers who leave. First prove a small group can't live without it. Then grow.

## Growth that feeds itself

The fastest-growing products work as **loops**, not funnels. Each customer's activity, whether content, invites, referrals or reinvested revenue, brings in the next customers. A funnel needs constant new input. A loop compounds.

## Money before revenue

Investors give a company money in exchange for a share of its future value. That money buys people, tools and time, so the company can grow faster than its own sales would allow, and survive the mistakes it will make along the way.

Funding isn't free. It costs ownership, and it brings expectations. But it is how a company stops being limited by its founders' own hours.

## Many people selling

Going to market is a team effort. Someone studies where the market is heading. Someone shapes the message. Someone opens doors, and someone closes the deal. Customer success makes sure the promise is kept, so customers stay and tell others.

A product reaches people because many minds work on getting it there.

## Try this

Take something you've built and write the five-part message for it: who, pain, promise, proof, path. Show it to one person who would use it, and ask what they'd do without it.

Their answer is your real competition.
