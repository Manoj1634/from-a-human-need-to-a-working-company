# From a Human Need to a Working Company

*Part 8 of From a Human Need to a Working Company*

---

This series started with one question: if coding is becoming a solved problem, what comes next?

Here is everything put together, as one path.

```
Human need → Value someone perceives → A solution, built in versions
     → Seats filled by specialists → Aim, story, funding and selling
     → Customers who stay and bring others ↻
```

## Most winners improved something old

Most great companies didn't solve a new problem. They solved an old one in a better way. There are three ways to do that:

1. **A better experience.** Before the iPhone was introduced in 2007, phones could already call, text, email and play music. Apple removed the friction: touch instead of tiny keys, the real web, and three devices in one. The App Store followed in July 2008 with about 500 apps and turned the phone into a platform.
2. **A better system.** Change how the parts fit together. Apple controlled hardware, software and services together. Microsoft licensed Windows to every PC maker. Clayton Christensen's research suggests that controlling the whole system tends to win while products still aren't good enough, and that open, modular systems win once they are.
3. **A new capability.** Rare shifts, like the internet, mobile chips and now AI models, make new solutions possible. Everyone else races to turn them into better experiences and better systems.

For engineers, this is the opportunity. A few labs own the new capability. The work for everyone else is turning it into a system and an experience that a specific customer can actually use.

## What keeps an advantage

Being better is a temporary edge. Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* names the ways a company keeps its advantage over time:

| Power | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Scale economies | Cost per unit falls as you grow |
| Network effects | Each new user makes it better for the others |
| Counter-positioning | A model incumbents can't copy without hurting themselves |
| Switching costs | Leaving is painful |
| Branding | People pay more for what you mean to them |
| Cornered resource | You own something scarce: data, talent, a license |
| Process power | Years of know-how a rival can't copy from a document |

Each one needs both a benefit and a barrier that stops others from copying it.

## Principles I'm taking with me

1. **Start at the need.** Trace every project to a real person and the job they need done.
2. **Define "done" per version.** Version 1 is the smallest useful thing, with one metric.
3. **Ship it like CI/CD.** Build, test, release, monitor, learn, repeat.
4. **Break it down.** Each piece gets one owner, one set of context, one check.
5. **Debug one piece at a time.** Find the broken part and change one thing.
6. **Save the lesson.** Every fix becomes a rule, an example or a tool.
7. **Make the value visible.** Raise belief with proof, and cut the customer's time and effort.
8. **Know the alternative.** Name what people use today, including doing nothing.
9. **Prove it before scaling.** A small group should hate to lose it first.
10. **Fill every seat.** Every seat has an owner: a person or an AI agent.

## Where this leaves me

I started this series as an engineer who could find a problem and build the fix. I end it with a clearer picture of everything around the fix: the need behind it, the value people perceive, the seats a company fills, and the work of getting it to people.

I'm still learning. But I'm now asking better questions in the work I do every day: not only "can we build this?" but also "who needs it, why will they care, and how will it reach them?"

If you've followed along, I hope you are too.
