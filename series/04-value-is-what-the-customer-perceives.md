# Value Is What the Customer Perceives

*Part 4 of From a Human Need to a Working Company*

---

Engineers tend to measure value by the work that went in. How hard was the problem? How elegant is the system? How many edge cases did we handle?

Customers don't see any of that. They see what changes for them.

This was one of the hardest ideas for me to accept. **A brilliant system that nobody understands is worth less than a simple one whose benefit is obvious.**

## The value equation

Alex Hormozi breaks value into four parts:

```
Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of success)
        ÷ (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice)
```

- **Dream outcome:** what the customer actually wants.
- **Perceived likelihood:** how much they believe they'll get it.
- **Time delay:** how long until they see the result.
- **Effort and sacrifice:** how much work it costs them.

Two of the four parts sit on the bottom. Many offers promise the same outcome. You win by being **more believable** and by getting the customer their result **sooner, with less effort on their side.**

The word that matters most is *perceived*. A system that could save a company a fortune is worth nothing to them if they don't believe it will work. Proof, examples, clear numbers and a small first win aren't marketing extras. They are part of the value.

## Why trading makes everyone richer

Picture one person living alone by a river. They fish, build their shelter, heal their own wounds, and entertain themselves. They work all day and stay poor, because their time is spread across every need.

Now picture a village. One person gets very good at baking and bakes for fifty families. Those families stop spending hours baking and spend that time on what *they* do well. Everyone ends up better off, even though nobody found any new resources.

In 1776, Adam Smith described this with a pin workshop. A handful of specialists, each doing one step, could make tens of thousands of pins a day. One untrained worker making whole pins alone might not make twenty. He gave three reasons:

1. People get more skilled when they repeat one task.
2. No time is lost switching between tasks.
3. Specialists invent better tools for their one step.

He also noticed a limit: **how far you can specialize depends on the size of your market.** The more people you can reach, the more you can focus. That is why reach matters so much.

## Creating value vs keeping it

Creating value and capturing it are two different skills.

Your price is the slice of the value you keep. The rest stays with the customer as their gain, and that is why they come back. A business that captures too little can't survive. A business that captures too much loses its customers.

As an engineer, I spent years creating value inside companies without thinking about the second part. Someone else decided the price, the contract and the model.

## Try this

Take something you've built. Score it on the four parts of the equation, from the customer's point of view, not yours:

- Do they clearly want the outcome?
- Do they believe it will work? What proof do they have?
- How long until they see a result?
- How much effort does it take them to get there?

The weakest score is usually where your next improvement should go, and it is often not in the code.
