# What Does It Take to Build a Company?

*Part 2 of From a Human Need to a Working Company*

---

I build systems for startups and for established companies. I make their work faster and cheaper by automating it and making it smarter.

Before this, I worked in fraud detection. We automated work that people used to do by hand. Then we made the systems smarter with machine learning and AI. Businesses saved time, and they made more money.

Across the companies I've worked with, I keep seeing the same thing. Many of them still run the way the cutting edge ran ten or fifteen years ago. Their tools and processes come from another era, and today that doesn't make sense. Whenever I join a company, I look for those gaps, suggest what should change, and build the solution. I'm good at that part.

That made me curious about the part I've never explored. Right now, what does it take to build a company? I don't mean just the solution. I mean everything around it: finding customers, selling to them, getting funded, hiring, and growing far past what one person can do.

I tried a small version of this once, back during my bachelor's. I looked for clients and sold digital marketing services. That was a different time, and I couldn't make it scale. Years later, after building inside many companies, the question came back to me, bigger this time.

**Why does a group of people working together do so much more than the same people working alone?**

## What companies have that one person doesn't

When I looked closely at the companies I've worked with, I kept finding the same four things.

1. **A need someone will pay for.** A company doesn't start from a solution. It starts from a person who needs something and pays to get it. *(Parts 3 and 4)*
2. **Someone in every seat.** Product, engineering, sales, marketing, finance, operations, customer success, legal. Each seat owns one question. *(Part 5)*
3. **Money before revenue.** Investors fund the company in exchange for a share of its future. That money buys people, tools and time, so the company can grow faster than its own sales allow. *(Part 7)*
4. **Many people selling.** Someone studies the market, someone shapes the message, someone opens doors, and someone closes the deal. *(Part 7)*

None of these is about code. Together, they are what turns a good solution into a business.

## What engineers already bring

The good news is that a lot of the thinking carries straight over.

- **We define "done" in versions.** Version 1 is the smallest thing that is truly useful, and each later version has its own finish line.
- **We ship through a pipeline.** Build, test, release, watch, learn, repeat. A business grows through the same loop.
- **We debug one piece at a time.** When something fails, we find the single broken part and change only that. A sales pitch that fails can be debugged the same way as a deploy that fails.

Part 6 goes deeper into this.

## What I want to understand better

1. How a company makes its value obvious to someone who doesn't care how it works.
2. How selling works, and how teams that sell are built.
3. How every seat gets filled, whether by a person or an AI agent.
4. How funding works, and when it makes sense.
5. How a solution goes from "one person can do this" to "a team can do this for many people."

The better I understand these, the better I can help the companies I work with see where their market is going and build the right things for it.

If you're an engineer, or you're thinking about building a company of your own, and this sounds familiar, the gap probably isn't your skill. You may simply never have sat in the other seats.
