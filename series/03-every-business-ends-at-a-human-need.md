# Every Business Ends at a Human Need

*Part 3 of From a Human Need to a Working Company*

---

As engineers, we usually start with the problem in front of us: a slow process, a broken pipeline, a manual task that should be automated.

A business starts further back. It starts with a person who needs something.

## The needs don't change

Take away the job titles and the dashboards, and every person is trying to do the same few things:

| Layer | Needs |
|---|---|
| **Survive** | Food, shelter, health |
| **Secure** | Enough resources for the future, so tomorrow isn't a fear |
| **Belong and enjoy** | Family, friends, bonding, entertainment |

The economist Manfred Max-Neef gives a fuller list of nine fundamental needs: subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, leisure, creation, identity and freedom. The last five matter as much as the first four. Courses sell understanding. Tools sell creation. Brands sell identity. Remote work sells freedom.

His sharpest point is that **the needs stay the same across cultures and centuries. What changes is how we meet them.** A letter, a phone call and a video chat all meet the need for affection. Each new technology is just a new way to satisfy an old need.

For anyone building something, that is reassuring. The need is solid ground. Your product is one way of meeting it, competing with every other way.

## People "hire" products

Clayton Christensen described the same idea from the buyer's side. People don't really buy products. They *hire* them to make progress in a specific situation.

His best-known example is a fast-food chain trying to sell more milkshakes. Researchers found that many were bought early in the morning by commuters. Those people weren't hiring a milkshake for its taste. They hired it to make a long, boring drive more bearable and to stay full until lunch. Its real competitors were a banana, a bagel, or simply being bored.

Every job a customer hires a product for has three sides:

- **Functional:** what it actually does
- **Emotional:** how it makes them feel
- **Social:** how it makes them look to others

## Following the chain

This is easy to see in consumer products. It's harder when you build B2B software, like many engineers do.

But the chain always ends at a person. A software company helps an insurance company. The insurance company helps hospitals. The hospitals help patients. The road is long, but it ends at a human with a need.

Many companies sit several steps away from that person. Each step in the chain is a business helping another business serve people better: faster, cheaper, or more reliably. Even the stock market fits in. Investors fund companies by buying a share of their future value, and the returns come back to people as savings and pensions, which is the "secure" need.

## The test

Before building anything, I now ask one question:

> **Whose human need does this ultimately serve, and does my part make it faster, cheaper, better, or easier to reach?**

If I can't answer it, the work is floating. If I can, I know where the value comes from, and why someone will pay for it.

## Try this

Pick something you're building right now. Trace it, one step at a time, until you reach a real person. Then name the job they are hiring it for, and its functional, emotional and social sides.

You may find the person at the end of the chain isn't who you expected.
