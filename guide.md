## 1. Every business ends at a human need

> **Core idea:** Needs are few and stable. Products are just the changing ways people satisfy them.

Take away the job titles and the dashboards, and every person is trying to stay alive, stay well, and stay connected.

| Layer | Needs |
|---|---|
| **Survive** | Food, shelter, health |
| **Secure** | Resources for the future, so tomorrow isn't a fear |
| **Belong and enjoy** | Family, friends, bonding, entertainment |

The economist Manfred Max-Neef gives a fuller list of nine: **subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, leisure, creation, identity, freedom.** Courses sell understanding, tools sell creation, brands sell identity, and remote work sells freedom.

### Needs vs satisfiers

Max-Neef's sharpest point is that needs are the same across cultures and centuries. What changes is the *satisfier*, meaning the specific way a need gets met. A letter, a phone call and a video chat all satisfy affection. For a founder, the need is the stable ground, and your product is just one satisfier competing with others.

### People "hire" products to make progress

Clayton Christensen's *Jobs to Be Done* says the same thing at the level of a single purchase. People don't buy a product; they hire it to make progress in a specific situation. His famous example is a fast-food chain's morning milkshakes. Commuters bought them to keep a long, boring drive occupied and to stay full until lunch. The real competitor wasn't another milkshake. It was a banana, a bagel, or boredom. Every job has three sides: **functional, emotional, social.**

**Test:** if you can't trace what you sell back to one of these needs, through a job someone is trying to get done, nobody needs it.

## 2. Value is what the customer perceives

> **Core idea:** Specialize, then trade. Whoever creates the most *perceived* value, and keeps a fair slice of it, gets rich.

### Why villages beat lone survivors

Alone by the river, one person fishes, builds, heals and entertains themselves. They stay poor because their time is spread across every need. In a village, the baker bakes for fifty families, and everyone gets time back.

Adam Smith described this in 1776 with a pin workshop. Ten specialists made tens of thousands of pins a day, where one untrained worker might not make twenty. He gave three reasons:
1. Skill grows with repetition.
2. No time is lost switching between tasks.
3. Specialists invent tools for their one step.

He also noted that **how far you can specialize is limited by the size of your market**, which is why reach matters.

### The value equation

A simple way to think about value is *need × number of people × how much better*. Alex Hormozi's version is sharper because it's about perception:

```
Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of success)
        ÷ (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice)
```

Two of the four levers are on the bottom. Many offers promise the same outcome. You win by being **more believable** (proof, guarantees, case studies) and by getting the customer the result **sooner, with less work on their side.**

### Create vs capture

Creating value and keeping it are different skills. Your price is the slice you keep. The rest is the customer's gain, and that's why they come back.

## 3. B2C, B2B and the stock market are one loop

> **Core idea:** No matter how many layers away you sit, the chain always ends at a human need.

```
Human need → B2C company → B2B tools for it → Picks & shovels
     ↑                                               ↓
Returns become people's savings  ←  Investors fund the chain
```

- A **B2C** company meets the need directly.
- A **B2B** company helps that company reach, serve or keep its customers better.
- **Infrastructure** companies help the B2B companies.
- The **stock market** buys slices of future value and funds the whole chain. The returns flow back to people as pensions and savings, which is the "resources for the future" need.
- **Sales** is the handshake at every link.

**The test for any link:** Whose human problem does this ultimately serve, and does my link make it faster, cheaper, better or easier to reach?

### Who captures the money in a chain

Joel Spolsky described a pattern in 2002: demand for a product goes up when the price of its complements goes down. So smart companies try to make their complements cheap and interchangeable. Microsoft licensed its operating system to every PC maker. PC hardware became a commodity, and the margin stayed with Windows. The same logic explains why some companies give away open AI models or free tools: they want the layer next to theirs to be cheap.

When you pick your link, ask: **which layer is the bottleneck everyone has to pass through, and which layers are commodities?**

## 4. The task model: roles, atoms and a frame

> **Core idea:** Performance = Skill × Context × Tools, inside Guardrails, measured by Evaluation.

A coaching class doesn't change your skill; it gives you context. AI works the same way. The model brings the skill. You supply the context and tools, set the limits, and check the result. That is context engineering.

| Layer | Question | Parts |
|---|---|---|
| **Frame** | Why, what, where (and when)? | Goal + metric, definition of done, owning department, deadline |
| **Roles** | Which role is acting? | Plan (trainer) → Build, Execute (performer) → Review, Validate (reviewer) |
| **Atoms** | What goes into the unit of work? | Context, tools, model, guardrails, eval |
| **Departments** | Where does it live? | Finance, Sales, Engineering, Product, People, Growth, Operations |

- **Build** creates a capability once (a tool, an agent, a template). **Execute** runs it again and again.
- **Review** asks "did we do it *right*?", meaning against standards. **Validate** asks "did we do the *right thing*?", meaning against the goal.

### What Anthropic's context-engineering guide adds

- **Context is a budget.** Models have limited attention, and their recall gets worse as the context grows (sometimes called *context rot*). Aim for the smallest set of high-signal information.
- **Write instructions at the right altitude.** Not brittle if-then rules, and not a vague "be helpful". Give clear heuristics.
- **Use few, clear tools.** If a human can't tell which tool fits a situation, the agent can't either.
- **A handful of varied examples** works better than a long list of edge cases.

### Task card

```
WHY:     goal + metric
WHAT:    deliverable + done-check
WHERE:   department + where context lives
WHEN:    deadline + what it waits on
VERSION: v1 scope → v2 → v3 (each with its own done-check)
ROLE:    Plan / Build / Execute / Review / Validate
ATOMS:   context | tools | model | guardrails | eval
GAP:     what's missing → how to get it → time/energy
```

## 5. Solving a problem in ten steps

> **Core idea:** Trace it, define it, find the real cause, check what you have, break it down, test small, fix one thing at a time, save every lesson.

```
solve(problem):
    need     = trace_to_human_need(problem)      # 1
    spec     = define(problem)                   # 2
    cause    = find_root_cause(spec)             # 3
    have     = inventory_resources()             # 4
    scope    = feasible_part(spec, have)         # 5
    tasks    = decompose_to_atoms(scope)         # 6
    for task in tasks:
        task.role, task.atoms = assign(task)     # 7
        result = run_smallest_test(task)         # 8
        while not passes(result, task.done_check):
            broken = troubleshoot(result)        # 9
            fix_one_thing(broken); result = rerun(task)
        save_lesson(task)                        # 10
    systematize(tasks)
```

- [ ] **1. Trace it to the need.** Keep asking "why does this matter?" until you reach a human need.
- [ ] **2. Define the problem.** *"Today it's X. We want Y. We'll know by Z."* If you can't write that sentence, don't start.
- [ ] **3. Find the root cause.** Keep asking "why" until you reach a process you can fix. Expect more than one cause.
- [ ] **4. Take inventory.** Skill, context, tools, money, time, people.
- [ ] **5. Check what's doable.** Do the part you can do now. The gap becomes its own task: get the missing piece.
- [ ] **6. Break it into atoms.** Each task gets one role, one context pack and one done-check. Group tiny tasks that share context.
- [ ] **7. Assign.** Give each task a role and its atoms. Decide whether AI, a person, or you does it.
- [ ] **8. Run the smallest test.** Try the cheapest version that could prove you wrong.
- [ ] **9. Troubleshoot.** Use the seven-places table below. Change one thing, then rerun.
- [ ] **10. Save the lesson and systematize.** Write the lesson down. A problem that keeps coming back becomes a playbook, then gets delegated, then gets automated.

### Ship it in versions, CI/CD style

"Done" is not one finish line. Phase the work into versions, each with its own done-check, and push every version through the same pipeline:

```
v1 (smallest useful)  →  v2 (expand)  →  v3 (scale)
   each: plan → build → test/eval → release → monitor → learn ↻
```

- **v1** solves the core problem for one user or one case, end to end.
- **Each version** passes the same checks before it ships: tests for code, evals for AI, a metric for the business.
- **Monitoring feeds the next version.** What breaks in production becomes the next version's input, the same way step 10 saves lessons.

This is the engineering version of a business's build-measure-learn loop: small releases that always work, rather than one big launch.

### A note on "five whys"

The technique comes from Toyota's production system. People who use it add two refinements. First, the number five is a nudge, not a rule; even Ohno's classic example has more links once you unpack it. Second, a real root cause usually points to a **process** that is broken or missing, not to a person. Some problems also have several root causes, so stop when you reach something a process change can fix.

## 6. Troubleshooting: seven places a task breaks

> **Core idea:** Find the broken piece, change one thing, rerun, check, save the lesson. Check the rows in order.

| What broke | What you see | Quick test | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | The output is "fine" but useless | Can you write "done" as one sentence you could check? | Rewrite the goal and the done-check |
| **Size** | Half-done, messy, drifting | Does it need more than one role or have more than one done-check? | Split it |
| **Context** | Wrong assumptions, generic output | Add one good example. Is it better right away? | Add rules, examples, sources |
| **Tools** | "I can't", made-up data, stuck | Does it need access it doesn't have? | Give access, or do that step by hand |
| **Model / skill** | Still wrong even with perfect context | Try a stronger model or an expert | Upgrade or train the performer |
| **Guardrails** | Unsafe, off-brand, overreaching | Did it break a rule nobody wrote down? | Write the rule down |
| **Eval** | Can't tell if it worked | Would two people agree on pass/fail? | Define the metric and a test case |

Goal and size problems look like every other problem, so rule them out first. Context is the most common real cause. The model or skill is usually the last thing to suspect.

**The fix loop:** name it in one line → change only that one thing → rerun the same input → check against the done-check → save the fix as a rule, example or tool.

## 7. Where to aim

> **Core idea:** What's scarce gets paid. What's abundant gets cheap.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How much does it hurt? | People pay to stop pain before they pay for nice-to-haves |
| Can they pay? | A need with no money behind it is charity |
| Can you reach them? | A perfect customer you can't reach is useless |
| Is it growing? | A growing market forgives mistakes |
| Why you? | What do you have that's hard to copy? |
| **What do they use today?** | That's your real competitor, often a spreadsheet or doing nothing |

AI is making writing, code and basic analysis abundant. Value is moving to what's still scarce: **trust, judgment, taste, owning an outcome, and distribution.**

### Will the advantage last?

Being better is a temporary edge. Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* names the seven ways a company keeps earning higher returns over time. Each one needs both a **benefit** and a **barrier** that stops rivals from copying it.

| Power | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Scale economies | Cost per unit falls as you grow |
| Network economies | Each new user makes it better for the others |
| Counter-positioning | Your model is one incumbents can't copy without hurting themselves |
| Switching costs | Leaving you is painful |
| Branding | People pay more because of what you mean to them |
| Cornered resource | You own something scarce: data, talent, a license |
| Process power | Years of know-how a rival can't copy from a document |

## 8. Communication: value nobody hears doesn't exist

> **Core idea:** The second baker had better bread, but worked in a back room with no sign out front. They went out of business.

Peter Drucker put it bluntly in 1954: the purpose of a business is "to create a customer." So a business has two basic functions, **marketing and innovation**, because those two produce results and everything else is cost. Communication isn't a department sitting next to the product. It's half the reason the company exists.

### The message, in order

1. **Who:** one specific person, not everyone
2. **Pain:** their problem, in their words
3. **Promise:** what changes for them
4. **Proof:** why they should believe you (the "perceived likelihood" lever)
5. **Path:** one next step

### Positioning comes before the message

April Dunford breaks positioning into parts you work out in order:

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what they'd use if you didn't exist
2. **Unique attributes:** what you have that the alternatives don't
3. **Value:** what those attributes do for the customer
4. **Best-fit customers:** who cares most about that value
5. **Market category:** the frame that makes your value obvious
6. *(Bonus)* **Relevant trends:** why it matters now

If the message isn't landing, the problem is often the positioning underneath it.

## 9. Winning when the problem is already solved

> **Core idea:** Most great companies solved an old problem in a better way, through a better experience, a better system, or a new capability.

| Way | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Better experience** | Remove the friction | Phones already did calls, email and music. The iPhone added touch instead of keys, the real web, and three devices in one. |
| **2. Better system** | Change how the parts fit together | Apple controlled hardware, software and services. Microsoft licensed Windows to every PC maker. |
| **3. New capability** | A rare shift makes new solutions possible | The internet, mobile chips, AI models. Others race to turn them into Ways 1 and 2. |

### The iPhone, by date

- **Jan 9, 2007:** Apple announced it as an iPod, a phone and an internet communicator in one device.
- **June 2007:** it went on sale, with only Apple-made apps.
- **July 10, 2008:** the App Store opened with about 500 apps, and the device became a platform.

The experience came first. The ecosystem that locked people in came eighteen months later.

### Reach vs control: when each one wins

Christensen's modularity theory says **integrated (vertical)** products win while products are still *not good enough*, because you can tune the whole system. Once products become *good enough*, **modular (horizontal)** products win on price and choice. That's how Windows PCs came to dominate in the 1990s.

Ben Thompson's counterpoint explains the iPhone: for consumers, the experience may never be "good enough," so integration can keep winning. **Figure out which situation your market is in before you choose.**

### Innovation isn't only about the product

Doblin built its *Ten Types of Innovation* from a study of about 2,000 innovations. It sorts them into three families:

- **Configuration:** profit model, network, structure, process
- **Offering:** product performance, product system
- **Experience:** service, channel, brand, customer engagement

Their finding: leaders combine several types, because a product on its own is easy to copy.

**For engineers:** AI is a capability shift owned by a few labs. For everyone else, the money is in turning it into a **system and an experience** that a specific customer can use. That is exactly what an AI engineer or an AI agency sells.

## 10. What makes a company grow

> **Core idea:** Solve a real need clearly better than the alternative, tell the right people, keep a fair share, and learn faster than anyone else.

| Driver | What it means | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Real need | It traces to a human need through a job | Can you name the job? |
| Product-market fit | A group of people would hate to lose it | 40%+ say "very disappointed" |
| Distribution | You can reach them, and they understand fast | Cost and time to win a customer |
| Value capture | A business model that keeps a fair slice | Margin per customer |
| Retention and loops | Customers stay and bring in the next ones | Does the retention curve flatten out? |
| Learning speed | Test small, fix one thing, save the lesson | Experiments per month |
| Durable power | An advantage with a barrier | Which of the 7 Powers do you have? |
| Runway | Enough cash to survive mistakes | Months of runway |

### Fit before scale

Sean Ellis's test asks users how they'd feel if they could no longer use the product. He benchmarked around a hundred startups. The ones where roughly **40% or more** answered "very disappointed" tended to grow, and the ones below that struggled. If you spend on growth before reaching that point, you mostly pay for customers who leave.

### Loops, not funnels

Reforge argues that the fastest-growing products work as **loops**: each user's output (content, invites, referrals, reinvested revenue) brings in the next users. A funnel needs constant new input, while a loop compounds. Paid ads can start the fire, but a loop keeps it burning.

## 11. A company is a body with seats

> **Core idea:** A person can solve a problem. A company is a system that solves it again and again, for many people at once, with every need owned by someone.

| Seat | Question it owns | Body part |
|---|---|---|
| CEO | Where are we going, and what do we bet on? | Brain |
| Strategy / Product / Growth | Where is the market moving, and why us? | Senses |
| CTO / Engineering | Can we build it, and will it keep working? | Hands, health |
| Marketing | Do the right people know about us and understand? | Voice |
| Sales / Trade | Will they exchange money for it? | Food |
| Customer Success | Did the value land, and will they stay? | Relationships |
| CFO / Finance | Can we afford it, and will we survive? | Resources for the future |
| Operations / People | Do we have the people, tools and process? | Family, bonding |
| **Legal / Security** | Are we protected from outside risk? | Shelter |

Sales brings money in from outside. Operations and People turn that money into capability inside. A company dies the same way a person does: when one of its needs goes unmet for too long.

### The loop around the customer

```
Strategy sees the problem → CEO bets → Finance funds → Ops staffs
   ↑                                                        ↓
Success delivers ← Sales trades ← Marketing tells ← Eng builds
```

Inside every seat, the same five roles run: someone **plans, builds, executes, reviews, validates.** Departments are *where* the work happens; roles are *how* it happens.

### Why a company makes more than a person

1. **Specialization:** each seat gets deeply good at one question.
2. **Parallel work:** many jobs move forward at the same time.
3. **Coverage:** no need gets forgotten, because someone owns each one.
4. **Compounding:** lessons become playbooks that outlast any one person.
5. **Leverage:** capital, tools and people multiply what one person can do.

### Two laws worth knowing

**A manager's output is the team's output.** Andy Grove defined it as the output of the organization they run plus the neighboring groups they influence. Leaders are measured by leverage, not by personal effort.

**The org chart becomes the product.** Melvin Conway observed that systems end up mirroring the communication structure of the groups that build them. If the seats don't talk to each other, the product has seams. This applies to multi-agent AI systems too: the way you split up agents shapes what they produce. The "inverse Conway" move is to design the teams around the system you want.

## 12. Principles I'm taking with me

> The principles I took from every section. Use them as a starting point for your own.

1. **Start at the need.** Trace every project to a human need and a job before building anything.
2. **Define "done" per version.** No work starts without a one-sentence done-check and a metric for v1; later versions get their own.
3. **Ship it like CI/CD.** Every version goes through build, test, release, monitor, learn. Small, working, often.
4. **Atomize.** Keep splitting until each task has one role, one context pack and one check.
5. **Debug the atom.** When something fails, find the broken piece and change one thing.
6. **Save the lesson.** Every fix becomes a rule, an example or a tool.
7. **Sell perceived value.** Raise certainty with proof, and cut the customer's time and effort.
8. **Know the alternative.** Name what customers use today, including doing nothing.
9. **Fit before scale.** Prove that a small group can't live without it before spending on growth.
10. **Build loops.** Prefer work whose output brings in the next customer.
11. **Choose reach or control.** Decide on purpose whether to integrate the whole stack or plug into someone else's.
12. **No orphan seats.** Every seat has an owner: me, a hire, or an AI agent.
13. **Protect the founder's atoms.** My health, my people and my runway are the company's first system.
